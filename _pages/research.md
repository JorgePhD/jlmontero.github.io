    <div>
      <div class="paper-title">
        How Fuel Subsidy Reductions Reshaped Fishing Activity:
        Evidence from China’s Policy Reform in the East China Sea
      </div>

      <div class="paper-authors">
        with
        <a href="https://www.udg.edu/ca/directori/pagina-personal?ID=113551">
          Jorge Marco
        </a>
      </div>

      <div class="paper-status">
        R&amp;R — Environmental and Resource Economics
      </div>
    </div>

    <a href="https://jorgephd.github.io/jlmontero.github.io/assets/papers/Fuel_Subsidies_Fishing.pdf"
       target="_blank"
       class="paper-button">
      PDF
    </a>

  </div>

  <details class="paper-abstract">
    <summary>Abstract</summary>

    <p>
      This paper examines how fuel subsidy reductions affect fishing activity, exploiting the 2016 reform of China’s fuel subsidy program in the East China Sea as a quasi-natural experiment. The reform reduced average subsidy payments by 69% and introduced an asymmetric distribution across vessel sizes, with larger vessels receiving 15.8% more support than smaller ones. We develop a theoretical framework to characterize fishers’ responses to differential subsidy cuts and test its predictions using Regression Discontinuity Design and Difference-in-Differences approaches. Our results show that while total fishing effort declined following the reform, unauthorized fishing increased by 19.3%, particularly in Taiwanese waters and disputed maritime zones. We find that larger vessels—which retained higher subsidy intensity—drove the expansion of unauthorized activity, whereas smaller vessels adjusted along the intensive margin over time. Consistent with our model, vessels facing larger relative subsidy reductions were 2.5% more likely to exit the sector, while those that remained intensified their fishing effort per unit of subsidy. These findings highlight a significant effort leakage across space and legal regimes, suggesting that subsidy reforms, while effective in lowering aggregate domestic effort, may induce unintended shifts toward unauthorized fishing and exacerbate geopolitical tensions.
    </p>

  </details>

      <div class="paper-title">
        Natural Resource Dependence, Livelihood Diversification and Well-Being in a Small-Scale Fishery: 
        Evidence from a Dynamic Household Production Model
      </div>

      <div class="paper-authors">
        with
        <a href="https://economia.uniandes.edu.co/maldonado">Jorge Maldonado</a>,
        Rocío Moreno-Sánchez,
        and <a href="https://fcds.org.co/equipo/miryam-elizabeth-vargas-morales/">
      Myriam Vargas</a>
      </div>

      <div class="paper-status">
        Under Review — Environment and Development Economics
      </div>

    </div>

    <a href="https://jorgephd.github.io/jlmontero.github.io/assets/papers/JEEM-D-25-00547.pdf"
       target="_blank"
       class="paper-button">
      PDF
    </a>

  </div>

  <details class="paper-abstract">
    <summary>Abstract</summary>

    <p>
      Small-scale fishing households in developing countries depend on natural resources both as a source of income and as a means of securing food and well-being. Understanding how these households respond to economic incentives is essential for designing policies that improve welfare while promoting environmental sustainability. This study develops a dynamic household production model integrating consumption, labor allocation, and harvesting decisions under openaccess conditions. Using household survey data from Barú in the Colombian Caribbean, we evaluate the effects of alternative policy interventions on well-being, fishing effort, and resource use. Results show that productive decisions respond strongly to economic incentives, whereas consumption decisions are less sensitive. While higher fish prices, productivity, and cost efficiency increase income, they also intensify harvesting pressure. In contrast, livelihood diversification improves well-being while reducing fishing effort. Resource depletion weakens most policy interventions, underscoring the importance of integrating household behavior and resource dynamics into environmental and development policy.
    </p>

  </details>

</div>

      <div class="paper-title">
        Illuminating the Shadows:
        Assessing Compliance and Effectiveness in Marine Protected Areas Using Satellite and Vessel Tracking Data
      </div>

      <div class="paper-authors">
        with
        <a href="https://economia.uniandes.edu.co/maldonado">Jorge Maldonado</a>
      </div>

      <div class="paper-status">
        Under Review — Marine Policy
      </div>

    </div>

    <a href="https://jorgephd.github.io/jlmontero.github.io/assets/papers/Esfuerzos_de_Pesca_y_MPAs.pdf"
       target="_blank"
       class="paper-button">
      PDF
    </a>

  </div>

  <details class="paper-abstract">
    <summary>Abstract</summary>

    <p>
      Marine Protected Areas (MPAs) are an essential instrument for marine conservation, aimed at promoting the sustainable use of marine resources. In this study, we examine the dynamics and behavior of industrial fishing vessels in relation to MPAs, leveraging extensive global fishing data. Using a regression discontinuity design, we evaluate vessel compliance by analyzing their presence within MPAs through Automatic Identification System (AIS) data and satellite imagery. The main findings indicate that MPAs significantly reduce industrial fishing activity within their boundaries, with a more pronounced reduction observed in MPAs with higher levels of fishing protection. These findings hold true when using both satellite imagery and AIS data. Differences arise when focusing on regions such as Indonesia, the Horn of Africa, and the Central Caribbean, regions characterized as a hotspot for piracy events, good fishing conditions, and consequently, non-publicly tracked vessels. We also find that fishing efforts decrease within less productive MPAs, while no significant effects are observed in the more productive ones. Additionally, larger MPAs and those located farther from piracy-prone regions are more effective in controlling fishing activity.
    </p>

  </details>

      <div class="paper-title">
        Unmasking the Threat to Property Rights:
        Unauthorized Fishing Activity during the COVID-19 Pandemic
      </div>

      <div class="paper-authors">
        with
        <a href="https://jobonill.wixsite.com/jorgebonilla">
          Jorge Bonilla
        </a>
      </div>

      <div class="paper-status">
        In Preparation
      </div>

    </div>

    <a href="https://jorgephd.github.io/jlmontero.github.io/assets/papers/Lockdown_and_Fishing.pdf"
       target="_blank"
       class="paper-button">
      PDF
    </a>

  </div>

  <details class="paper-abstract">
    <summary>Abstract</summary>

    <p>
      The global health crisis has disrupted economic activities and posed significant challenges to fisheries management, control, and surveillance. In this paper, we examine the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on property rights in the context of unauthorized fishing activity providing theoretical and empirical evidences. This study investigates to what extent the pandemic has led to an increase in unauthorized fishing, potentially undermining existing property rights systems. To do so, we compile a comprehensive database with weekly, country-level fishing effort data for 146 countries before and during the COVID-19 lockdowns and international fishing access agreements data. We employ Differences in Differences approaches to shed light on the consequences of the pandemic for marine resource governance. The findings indicate that the stringency of the restrictions led to a decrease in authorized fishing efforts. However, unauthorized fishing hours increased, but we did not find any effects on the number of vessels engaged in unauthorized fishing due to the onset of the lockdowns. The increase in unauthorized fishing hours was higher in low- and middle-income countries, and in areas with a higher biodiversity index. These results inform the design of control policies by understanding the motivations of fishermen in low-monitoring scenarios.
    </p>

  </details>

    <div>
      <div class="paper-title">
        Deforestation in the Colombian Amazon Biome: Socioeconomic and Socio-Environmental Conflict Drivers
      </div>
      <div class="paper-authors">
        <a href="https://economia.uniandes.edu.co/maldonado" target="_blank">Jorge Maldonado</a> · Rocío Moreno-Sánchez · <strong>Jorge Montero-Mestre</strong> 
      </div>
      <div class="paper-status">
        World Development — Forthcoming
      </div>
    </div>
    <a href="https://jorgephd.github.io/jlmontero.github.io/assets/papers/Amazon_SDGN.pdf" target="_blank" class="paper-button">
      PDF
    </a>
  </div>

  <details class="paper-abstract">
    <summary>Abstract</summary>
    <p>
      Colombia is one of the world’s most biodiverse countries, home to over 10% of the planet’s biodiversity. Much of this richness is found in the Amazon biome, which spans over 43% of the country’s land surface. Despite its ecological importance, the region is subject to growing threats from deforestation, leading to biodiversity loss and degradation of ecosystem services. Key drivers of this deforestation include socio-environmental conflicts, such as land conversion, coca cultivation, and illegal mining, all of which are compounded by Colombia’s longstanding internal armed conflict. Socioeconomic conditions in local communities further reinforce these drivers, yet limited access to reliable data constrains understanding and has led to a gap in the literature. This study examines the links between socioeconomic factors, socio-environmental conflicts, and deforestation across the Amazon biome in Colombia. We construct a broad set of indicators, and use econometric methods and machine learning (ML) techniques to identify the main drivers of forest loss. The results show that while the contribution of coca cultivation to deforestation has declined, land grabbing has emerged as a leading driver. Mining causes significant disturbances to the Amazon biome, but its link to deforestation is marginal. Other consistent predictors include poverty and the presence of basic trails and roads. The analysis also confirms that the northwestern zone remains the most threatened subregion, and that forest conservation is positively associated with the presence of Indigenous communities.
    </p>
  </details>
